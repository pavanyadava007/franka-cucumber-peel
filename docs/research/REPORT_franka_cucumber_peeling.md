# Teaching a Franka to peel cucumbers

A master's student can get a Franka FR3 peeling cucumbers with a handheld peeler in about six months by reproducing **Reactive Diffusion Policy (RDP, RSS 2025)** on the real robot and using simulation as a force-control companion, not as the main data source. A ten-persona review committee ranked RDP first among 23 candidates (**85.5 of 100, first for 9 of 10 reviewers**): it peels a cucumber with a grasped peeler at a score of **0.95 against 0.44** for a vision-only Diffusion Policy, it ships MIT-licensed code, data and checkpoints with a Franka setup guide, and its best variant uses a wrench estimated from joint torques, the same kind of signal a Franka streams at 1 kHz. The evidence points to one pipeline: CRISP Cartesian impedance control on franka_ros2 with error clipping, a rigid peeler mounted on the flange behind a wrist force/torque sensor, a spiked fixture that turns the cucumber between strokes, 60 to 100 single-stroke SpaceMouse demos recorded with a live force display, RDP trained on the cloud L4, and a MuJoCo digital twin that models the skin as a removable thickness grid instead of fracture physics. Nobody has published sim-to-real peeling, and no peer-reviewed paper reports learned force-aware cucumber peeling on a Franka (two arXiv-only 2026 preprints do, without simulation). A sim-trained residual force corrector, tested against a real-data-only RDP baseline, is therefore a real thesis contribution, and the baseline is a result you get either way. The force sensor dominates the add-on budget (Bota SensONE, **CHF 4,882.50** list), while each wrist camera costs about **EUR 330 to 415**. The local GTX 1650 is only good for MuJoCo development; every learned policy in the ranking needs the L4 or a bigger GPU to train.

## Force feedback roughly doubles peeling scores, but almost none of it ran on a Franka

Since 2025, cucumber peeling has become a de facto benchmark for force- and tactile-aware imitation learning. The results agree on one point: closing a loop on force roughly doubles what vision alone achieves. **RDP's force variant scores 0.95 and Diffusion Policy 0.44** on normalized peel length. With perturbations after contact the gap widens to 0.88 against 0.19 ([RDP arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02881v3)). **FoAR scores 0.756 against 0.386** for Diffusion Policy over 20 trials ([FoAR arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.15753v2)). On zucchini, ForceMimic's HybridIL makes a continuous peel longer than 10 cm in **85% of trials against 55%** for raw Diffusion Policy ([ForceMimic arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.07554v3)). ForceVLA raises peel length per stroke from 10.27 cm to 14.12 cm and cuts the strokes needed to clean a cucumber from 14 to 7 ([ForceVLA arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22159)). TouchGuide's tactile steering lifts a pi0.5 policy to a 0.975 score on bimanual peeling ([TouchGuide RSS 2026](https://www.roboticsproceedings.org/rss22/p078.pdf)). The physical reason is the same in every paper. Vision-only policies lose contact or press too hard. ForceMimic's Diffusion Policy baseline averaged **about 20 N with peaks above 40 N** and damaged the produce, while HybridIL held **about 9 N** and its demonstration data sat around 10 N ([ForceMimic arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.07554)). That gives the controller a target band. The FORCA agent's 4.5 to 7.5 N band with a 30 N limit is a second reference ([FORCA arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.31841)).

Almost all of this ran on Flexiv Rizon, xArm, ARX, UR or Kinova arms. The Franka record is thin. The best evidence is a mix of non-learned systems and preprints without code. MORPHeus (Cornell, ICRA 2024) peels with a Franka Panda under Cartesian impedance, with the food held in an assistive cutting board. Cucumber success jumps from **2/10 at low stiffness to 10/10 at medium and high stiffness**, but the system is not a learned policy and needs manual rotation between strokes ([MORPHeus arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.06570)). Any-ttach (RSS 2026 workshop) runs Diffusion Policy on an FR3 with a peeler locked into a quick-swap coupling. It reaches 9/15 with no force sensing, and the authors report that holding the peeler by grasping it "could not be demonstrated" ([Any-ttach PDF](https://dex-manipulation.github.io/rss2026/pdfs/8_Any_ttach_Quick_End_effector.pdf)). The strongest on-target number is **FAWAM's 19/20 on an FR3 with a wrist ATI Axia80 sensor**, against 8/20 for pi0.5. But FAWAM is arXiv-only, has no code and trained on 8 A100s ([FAWAM arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08555v3)). Dream-Tac reaches 85% on a Panda with fingertip tactile sensors. It is also arXiv-only and is built on a 2B-parameter video world model trained on H200-class hardware ([Dream-Tac arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08737)).

Two cautions shape everything that follows. First, **peeling metrics are not comparable across papers**. Papers report normalized peel length (RDP, FoAR), strip-length thresholds of 5 cm (Dream-Tac), 8 cm ([BiTAT](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/17/8383)) or 10 cm (ForceMimic), peel length per stroke (ForceVLA), or a 0 to 9 human score (Berkeley's knife paper). Headline numbers also shrink when another lab runs the method. RDP scored 0.740 inside TouchGuide's setup ([TouchGuide PDF](https://www.roboticsproceedings.org/rss22/p078.pdf)), and OmniVTA found that RDP's reactive policy "often produces overly strong contacts that damage the sensors" ([OmniVTA arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.19201)). Second, **no paper simulates the peel itself**. The Berkeley knife-peeling authors write that simulated cutting "remain[s] difficult to scale beyond simple insertion or cutting tasks" for heterogeneous produce ([How to Peel arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.03280v1)). The only simulation inside a peeling system is the MIT/TRI work, which trained in-hand reorientation of the vegetable in Isaac Gym while a Franka held the peeler under teleoperation or a scripted path ([Vegetable Peeling arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.07884v1)).

## A ten-reviewer committee ranks RDP first among 23 candidates

### How the scoring works

The committee scored 23 candidate papers from 2024 to 2026: every peeling paper in the research notes, plus the force-control, teleoperation and sim-to-real papers that a Franka pipeline would actually depend on. Each paper got a 0 to 5 score on each of the eight criteria in the brief. **These criterion scores are this report's judgement from the evidence, not numbers from the papers.** The anchors were fixed before scoring. Peeling relevance gives 5 to a learned cucumber policy using a handheld peeler and 3 to surface-removal analogues such as wiping or scraping. Venue gives 5 when proceedings or the publisher page confirm a listed venue, 4 when only an arXiv comment or project BibTeX states it, 2 to a workshop and 0 to 1 to arXiv-only work. Franka fit gives 5 to results on FR3 hardware, and force/tactile gives 5 when force closes the control loop and an ablation shows the gain. Results reward 20 or more trials, perturbation tests and clear margins. Openness gives 5 to a permissive licence with code, data, checkpoints and a hardware guide, and at most 2 to code without a licence. L4 reproduction cost gives 5 when training fits easily on one L4 and 1 when it needs a multi-A100 or H200 node. Sim-to-real gives 5 to a sim-trained contact or material-removal policy that worked on a real robot.

Ten personas weighted these criteria differently. Each persona's weighted score was scaled to 100, and the committee score is the mean across personas. Because the mean is linear, the committee's effective weights work out to: results 15%, peeling relevance and force/tactile 14.5% each, openness 13.5%, Franka fit 12.5%, venue 11%, sim-to-real 10% and L4 cost 9%.

### What the ranking says

RDP is the clearest result. It tops every persona except the sim-to-real researcher, who prefers the chicken-deboning paper and still places RDP fifth. It also stays first under two sensitivity checks. With equal weights it scores 82.5, ahead of HIL-SERL at 77.5 and MORPHeus at 72.5. With a hard peeling gate (relevance of 4 or more), the top ten becomes RDP, MORPHeus, ForceMimic, TouchGuide, ForceVLA, FoAR, How to Peel (arXiv-only), FAWAM (arXiv-only), Dream-Tac (arXiv-only) and RTR (ICML 2026). Below second place the scores are close. **Places 6 to 10 sit within 1.7 points**, so treat them as a tie rather than an order.

TouchGuide splits the committee more than any other paper (58.0 to 87.0). Venue and evaluation reviewers rate it near the top. Open-science and budget reviewers mark it down because the notes found no code release and the paper relies on bimanual ARX5 arms with Xense tactile sensors.

The list holds two kinds of papers, and a thesis needs both. Peeling-evidence papers (RDP, MORPHeus, ForceMimic, TouchGuide, ForceVLA, FoAR) show what works. Pipeline enablers (HIL-SERL, FACTR, ACP, ImplicitRDP) supply the Franka control, teleoperation and action interfaces.

The most telling result is the gap. **No sim-to-real paper makes the top ten**, because none of them peels. The best sim templates (deboning, SliceIt!, FR3 scraping) sit at places 11 to 13. FAWAM shows the tension between relevance and reproducibility. It is the single most on-target result (FR3, wrist F/T, cucumber, 19/20), but with no code and an 8-A100 training budget it finishes 17th. The other arXiv-only standouts fall for similar reasons: How to Peel has no licence, and Dream-Tac needs H200-class training.

## Reactive Diffusion Policy is the paper to build on and reproduce

RDP wins on fit, not just on score. It solves the exact task: grasp a peeler, approach a cucumber, peel, and recover from perturbations both before and after contact. It splits control into a slow latent diffusion policy that emits action chunks at 1 to 2 Hz and a fast asymmetric-tokenizer decoder that reacts to force or tactile input at 24 Hz ([RDP arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02881v3)). Its best variant uses no external sensor. It uses the **TCP wrench estimated from joint torques at 120 Hz**, which is the same kind of signal the Franka Control Interface streams at 1 kHz as its "estimation of externally applied forces" ([FR3 brochure](https://franka.de/hubfs/Brochure_Franka%20Research%203_February%202026_English.pdf)). So a student can reproduce the headline variant before buying a force sensor.

The release is the most complete of any peeling paper. It has an MIT licence ([LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/xiaoxiaoxh/reactive_diffusion_policy/main/LICENSE)), the dataset and checkpoints on Hugging Face ([HF dataset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/WendiChen/reactive_diffusion_policy_dataset)), and a Franka setup document that recommends a separate real-time PC running the Franka server as close to 1000 Hz as possible ([Franka setup doc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/xiaoxiaoxh/reactive_diffusion_policy/main/docs/franka_setup_instructions.md)). The authors' TactAR data-quality study also tells you what to get right in data collection. Force feedback during teleoperation raised operators' normalized peel length from 0.72 to 0.91, and training on demos collected without feedback **dropped RDP's peeling score by more than 30%** ([RDP arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02881v3)). And because later papers use RDP as a baseline, your FR3 result will have outside points of comparison.

The Franka port is the weak spot, and the issue tracker shows where it will hurt. Users hit conflicts between ROS 2 Humble on Python 3.10 and Polymetis on Python 3.8, and the newer Deoxys server is "not yet fully validated on a physical robot" ([issue #31](https://github.com/xiaoxiaoxh/reactive_diffusion_policy/issues/31)). The Franka server once put 7-D joint torques into the 6-D TCP-wrench field. The maintainer confirmed this, and PR #32 converts it through the Jacobian ([issue #28](https://github.com/xiaoxiaoxh/reactive_diffusion_policy/issues/28)). On non-Flexiv arms, actions jitter unless a high-rate interpolator runs at 1000 Hz or more ([issue #6](https://github.com/xiaoxiaoxh/reactive_diffusion_policy/issues/6)). One user reports that training the latent diffusion stage needs **more than 20 GB of free VRAM** ([issue #10](https://github.com/xiaoxiaoxh/reactive_diffusion_policy/issues/10)). That fits a 24 GB L4, but only just.

There is also a structural mismatch. RDP outputs relative end-effector poses with no stiffness or wrench term, and in the paper a human holds the cucumber in midair. That is the opposite of the fixtured, single-arm setup a Franka lab would build. All of these problems can be fixed, and fixing them is ordinary thesis engineering: the 1 kHz interpolation becomes the impedance controller's job, the wrench comes from the FCI estimate, and the fixture removes the human from the loop.

The strongest alternatives each lose on one decisive point. **ForceMimic/HybridIL** is the only peeling policy that predicts a wrench for a hybrid force-position controller, and it is MIT-licensed, but it peels zucchini, its execution relies on Flexiv's built-in hybrid force-position primitives, and its capture device uses the discontinued T265 and L515 sensors ([forcecapture README](https://github.com/ForceMimic/forcecapture)). **FoAR** needs only position control and has a strong cucumber result, but its repository has no licence file ([FoAR GitHub](https://github.com/Alan-Heoooh/FoAR)), so you cannot legally build a thesis on it without written permission. **How to Peel with a Knife** has the best data-collection evidence and runs on a torque-controlled single arm close to a Franka, but it uses a knife, is arXiv-only, and also has no licence ([how-to-peel GitHub](https://github.com/ToruOwO/how-to-peel)). **HIL-SERL** is the most Franka-native option: it was run on a Panda and an FR3 ([robot infra README](https://github.com/rail-berkeley/hil-serl/blob/main/serl_robot_infra/README.md)), and real-world RL took average success from 49.7% to 100% on 13 tasks ([HIL-SERL arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21845v3)), but it has no peeling evidence and is tied to ROS 1 Noetic. **FAWAM** is on the right robot, but there is nothing to download.

### A MuJoCo twin with a removable skin grid, not fracture physics

No mainstream simulator offers "a blade removes a thin skin layer" out of the box. MuJoCo flex supports deformable capsules, triangles and tetrahedra, but its documentation describes no cutting, fracture or topology change ([MuJoCo modeling docs](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/modeling.html)). Isaac Lab 3.0 simulates soft bodies with FEM in PhysX and with an experimental VBD backend in Newton. These deform but do not separate material ([Isaac Lab deformables](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/release/3.0.0/source/tutorials/01_assets/run_deformable_object.html)). Of the mainstream simulators, only the MPM family separates material natively. Genesis ships a "cut dragon" rigid-plus-MPM coupling example ([Genesis GitHub](https://github.com/genesis-embodied-ai/genesis-world)), but users report abnormal cutting of an MPM sheet by a cutter mesh ([issue #351](https://github.com/genesis-embodied-ai/genesis-world/issues/351)). An MPM orange-peel fracture model needed about 50,000 particles and about 156 s per run, with no blade and no robot in the scene ([food fracture arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.16870)).

Material-removal work that did transfer to real robots used simple calibrated removal rules instead of full fracture physics. The closest template runs on the target robot. An FR3 scraping study in MuJoCo modelled the material as a few hundred spheres, each with a dislodgement-force threshold drawn from Perlin noise. Its PPO policy acted at 10 Hz over a 500 Hz Cartesian impedance controller and transferred zero-shot at **75.3% average relative success against 64.44%** for a fixed-wrench baseline ([FR3 scraping arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.10979v1)). RoboFin3D removed material from an SDF with a Preston-type rule driven by contact pressure. After calibration its removed-volume error was 20.2 to 26.7% ([RoboFin3D arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.37560)). A tool-use study on a Franka Panda pretrained surface-following trajectories in MuJoCo and reached **93.54% wiped area against 72.74%** from demonstrations alone ([Edinburgh/Sony arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.13200)).

The minimum viable cucumber model follows from these precedents; the design is this report's synthesis, not a published method. The cucumber is a rigid capsule or scanned mesh, clamped the way the real fixture holds it, and its skin is a 2-D grid over axial position and angle that stores remaining skin thickness. The peeler blade is a rigid edge in MuJoCo's soft contact. At each step, cells under the blade lose thickness when normal force exceeds a per-cell threshold and blade tilt is inside a "bite" window, with removal depth tied to how far the blade engages. Going deeper than the skin counts as a gouge (the knife paper's "too low" failure), cells the blade passes over without engaging count as misses ("too high"), and coverage is the fraction of cells at zero thickness. Randomize radius, taper and curvature (the tapered end is where RDP needed a downward correction), surface friction, contact stiffness, the removal thresholds and the clamp pose.

Observations should use normalized, discretized force. The deboning paper found that "absolute contact dynamics are hard to model" and transferred zero-shot with this trick ([deboning arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15376v2)).

Calibration needs real strokes, because the notes found no published mechanical parameters for cucumber skin. SliceIt! calibrated its cutting simulator from "as few as one slice motion" per food, cucumber included ([SliceIt! arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.02569v2)). DiSECt calibrated against Panda joint-torque force data while cutting cucumbers ([DiSECt arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.10263)). The same approach works here: a handful of scripted constant-speed strokes at several depths, logging normal force, tangential drag, blade tilt and stroke speed, and weighing the peel from each.

Two design rules matter most for transfer. Run the same impedance law in simulation as on the robot. And **keep the sim-trained part vision-free**: a residual corrector that sees only force, proprioception and blade pose relative to the surface, and adjusts the normal-axis target depth and stiffness at 10 Hz on top of RDP or a scripted stroke. FAWAM already shows a 10 Hz force-guided residual corrector working on an FR3, though it was trained on real interventions ([FAWAM arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08555v3)). The deboning paper shows a residual PPO policy with small MLPs, trained in simulation, lifting real-chicken success **from 0.10 to 0.50** ([deboning arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15376v2)).

Keep expectations realistic. In DexNinja's slicing study, sim-only training (34%) roughly matched real-only (31%), and combining the two gave 50%. Cutting-physics mismatch caused 17 of its 50 failures ([DexNinja arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.17208)). Separately, sim-and-real co-training improved real performance by an average of 38% ([co-training arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.24361)).

### Sixty to one hundred SpaceMouse strokes with a force display

The best published comparison of collection methods comes from Berkeley's knife peeling on cucumbers. SpaceMouse teleoperation on a compliant controller gave **100% usable demos with a mean score of 8.5 at 46 s each**. VR managed 20%. Kinesthetic teaching looked fine, but its replays scored 0% because the compliance parameters were ambiguous ([How to Peel arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.03280v1)). In the same paper, 50 single-stroke cucumber demos from about 8 cucumbers gave 100% in-category success.

Other papers report similar demo counts: RDP used 60, FoAR 50, FAWAM 90 plus 20 intervention episodes, and Dream-Tac 100 ([FAWAM arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08555v3), [Dream-Tac arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08737)). Because RDP's own study showed that operator force feedback matters, add a live force bar to the SpaceMouse workflow.

Two upgrades exist. The first is RDP's TactAR app on a Meta Quest 3 (about USD 500), which gives exact parity with the paper ([RDP arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02881v3)). The second is a FACTR force-feedback leader arm, about USD 1,229.95 in parts, Apache-2.0 licensed, and the device FAWAM used for FR3 cucumber peeling ([FACTR arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.17432v2)). FACTR's Franka follower node is unreleased ([factr issue #1](https://github.com/RaindragonD/factr/issues/1)), but CRISP already provides leader-follower teleoperation with force feedback at about 30 Hz and records in LeRobot format ([CRISP arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.06819)).

At the knife paper's rate of about six strokes per cucumber, 100 demos consume roughly 17 cucumbers. This report's estimate for evaluation is about 20 more for 120 single-stroke trials, but one cucumber per trial if trials run to full coverage.

### RDP on a clipped Cartesian impedance controller, then a stiffness head

For an FR3, the best-supported control stack is **CRISP on franka_ros2 with libfranka 0.19.0 and a PREEMPT_RT kernel**. CRISP runs Cartesian impedance and operational-space control at 1 kHz on the joint-torque interface. It tracks the latest target published at any rate, exposes a target-wrench term and runtime stiffness parameters, and clips tracking error. On a real FR3 it held a 0.81 mm steady-state error with clipping and tracked Diffusion Policy at about 30 Hz ([CRISP arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.06819)). libfranka recommends a real-time kernel ([libfranka GitHub](https://github.com/frankarobotics/libfranka)), and franka_ros2 calls one "essential" ([franka_ros2 GitHub](https://github.com/frankarobotics/franka_ros2)). The real-time kernel conflicts with CUDA on the same machine ([serl_franka_controllers](https://github.com/rail-berkeley/serl_franka_controllers)), so budget for two PCs: one for real-time control and one with a GPU.

Franka lists both Jazzy and Humble distributions of franka_ros2 ([compatibility](https://frankarobotics.github.io/docs/compatibility.html)), and CRISP supports all ROS 2 distributions from a single branch ([crisp_controllers](https://github.com/utiasDSL/crisp_controllers)). The student can therefore stay on RDP's Ubuntu 22.04, ROS 2 Humble and PyTorch 1.13.1 stack ([RDP GitHub](https://github.com/xiaoxiaoxh/reactive_diffusion_policy)) and replace RDP's Polymetis server with CRISP as the 1 kHz interpolator.

Start with fixed stiffness. UMI's Franka path used 750 N/m translational Cartesian stiffness at 1000 Hz ([UMI controller](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/real-stanford/universal_manipulation_interface/main/umi/real_world/franka_interpolation_controller.py)). At that stiffness, placing the target 8 mm below the cucumber surface produces about 6 N of normal force, inside FORCA's band, and 13 mm produces about 10 N, ForceMimic's level. Clipping the pose error at 30 mm caps the spring force near 22.5 N, below FORCA's 30 N limit. (These three figures are this report's arithmetic.) This is the bounded-error idea behind HIL-SERL's reference limiting ([HIL-SERL arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21845v3)).

The thesis-grade extension is an action head that outputs a virtual target plus stiffness. ACP outputs a reference pose, a virtual target and a stiffness value, and reached 93.75% on vase wiping against 43.75% for a compliant baseline ([ACP arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.09309v2)). ImplicitRDP sets the virtual target to the measured pose plus K^-1 times the external force, with k between 200 and 10,000 N/m ([ImplicitRDP arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.10946v2)). That law, F = K(x_target - x) - D·ẋ, is exactly what a Franka Cartesian impedance controller computes. A secondary review gives the FR3's translational stiffness range as 10 to 3,000 N/m ([Anton Robots review](https://antonrobots.com/franka-research-3-review/)), so cap any learned stiffness below ACP's UR-derived ceiling of about 5,000 N/m ([ACP issue #2](https://github.com/yifan-hou/adaptive_compliance_policy/issues/2)). Whether CRISP's parameter services can change stiffness at the policy's 10 to 30 Hz rate is not documented, so plan to add a stiffness field to the target message. No paper in the notes has published an RDP-style slow/fast policy with a virtual-target and stiffness head on a peeling task.

For force sensing, the Panda datasheet gives **0.8 N relative force accuracy and 0.035 N RMS noise** ([Franka datasheet](https://download.franka.de/Datasheet-EN.pdf)). That is about 8 to 9% of a 9 to 10 N peel force: enough for contact logic and as a policy feature, but marginal for regulating peel thickness. The FR3 brochure gives no numeric accuracy. RDP itself notes that estimated wrenches are noisier than a wrist sensor such as an ATI Mini45 ([RDP arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02881v3)). Phase 1 should log the FCI estimate with a correct load model for the peeler and adapter. Phase 2 should add a wrist sensor and record both signals on the same strokes. No paper has published that comparison, so it is a small result in its own right.

On a Panda, the path differs. Vendor support has stopped: libfranka 0.9.2 is the last version for the Franka Emika Robot ([panda-python](https://pypi.org/project/panda-python/)). External Cartesian commands are "prone to vibrations and to protective stops", and the community has kept the arm alive with a Jazzy port and multipanda_ros2 ([Panda alive arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.19740)). Use those, or ROS 1 with serl_franka_controllers, and do not mix FR3 instructions into a Panda setup.

### A rigid peeler mount, an indexing fixture and one expensive sensor

Mount the peeler rigidly, not in the gripper. Any-ttach could not make a grasped peeler work on an FR3. ForceMimic, the Science Robotics diffused-fields work and the knife paper all used rigid mounts ([Diffused fields PDF](https://calinon.ch/papers/Bilaloglu-ScienceRobotics2026.pdf), [How to Peel arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.03280v1)). The recommended stack is flange, then F/T sensor, then a printed or aluminium adapter, then a peeler clamp. The Bota SensONE uses an ISO 9409-1-50-4M6 flange ([Bota SensONE](https://botasys.com/force-torque-sensors/sensone/)), and the FR3 payload is 3 kg ([FR3 product page](https://franka.de/franka-research-3)). Put compliance in the controller, not in the mount, so that simulation and robot share one compliance model.

Copy the knife paper's camera finding. Of its two D405 wrist cameras, the one looking at the contact just before the blade mattered more, because it was less occluded ([How to Peel arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.03280v1)). The D405 works best at 7 to 50 cm ([DigiKey](https://www.digikey.de/de/product-highlight/i/intel-realsense/d405-depth-camera)).

For holding the cucumber, MORPHeus is the Franka precedent. It used a board with a moving jaw or spikes, fixed to the table with suction cups, and found that pegs can occlude the middle of the food ([MORPHeus arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.06570v2)). A two-spike, between-centres holder that turns the cucumber 30 to 45 degrees between strokes generalizes that design to a long cylinder. This is an inference, not a published comparison.

Layer the safety from the policy outward: bounds on policy actions and a workspace box, then clipped impedance error, then torque-rate limits, then Franka collision thresholds set with `setCollisionBehavior` above peel forces but well below the levels that permit hard impacts ([libfranka reference](https://frankarobotics.github.io/libfranka/latest/classfranka_1_1Robot.html)), then an enable switch held by the operator, and finally cut-resistant gloves for anyone loading produce. FDM-printed parts are not automatically food-safe ([Creality](https://www.creality.com/blog/is-3d-printed-pla-food-safe)), so treat experiment produce as non-food. The arm is rated IP40 per a secondary source, so use a drip tray.

### Twenty trials per condition and six metrics from the literature

Because no standard peeling benchmark exists, report the union of metrics so the result can be compared with every group:

| Metric | Definition | Precedent |
|---|---|---|
| Normalized peel length | Peeled length over cucumber length, normalized by the demo average | RDP, FoAR |
| Strip success | Continuous strip longer than 5, 8 and 10 cm (report all three) | Dream-Tac, BiTAT, ForceMimic |
| Per-stroke length and strokes to clean | cm per stroke; strokes to full coverage | ForceVLA |
| Coverage | Segmentation of skin against flesh after K strokes while turning the cucumber | New; SAM2 masks used in the knife paper |
| Flesh waste | Mass before minus mass after minus peel mass; peel mass per cm² as a thickness proxy | New; not reported anywhere |
| Force quality and safety | Mean and std of normal force, stable-contact ratio, gouges, misses, reflex stops | RDP, ForceMimic, knife paper |

Run 20 trials per condition. That matches FoAR, ForceMimic, TouchGuide and FAWAM, and it is the minimum that separates force-aware from vision-only policies. Twenty trials cannot separate two force-aware variants. This report's Wilson 95% intervals show why: FAWAM's 19/20 spans 0.76 to 0.99, TA-VLA's 17/20 spans 0.64 to 0.95, ForceVLA's 14/20 spans 0.48 to 0.85, and only pi0.5's 8/20 (0.22 to 0.61) clearly falls below the others ([FAWAM arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08555v3)). For close comparisons, use paired tests on the continuous metrics or run 30 to 40 trials.

Test five conditions: the nominal fixture pose; diameter and curvature bins measured with calipers; height or pose shifts (FAWAM varied table height, and lowering the table by 3 cm dropped TAO-Force from 96.7% to 86.7% ([TAO-Force arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.18497))); a push after contact, as in RDP; and zucchini as out-of-distribution produce, where the knife paper's cucumber policy managed 50% ([How to Peel arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.03280v1)). Compare six methods. The floor is a scripted point-cloud stroke under impedance, following the MIT recipe of segmenting the vegetable, taking its PCA long axis and fitting a spline ([Vegetable Peeling arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.07884v1)); expect it to do poorly, since the knife paper's heuristic planner reached only 20%. Above it sit vision-only Diffusion Policy, RDP with the estimated wrench, RDP with the F/T sensor, the sim-trained residual corrector run zero-shot, and RDP co-trained with simulation data.

### The GTX 1650 develops, the L4 trains, the robot PC runs the policy

The GTX 1650 is a Turing card with compute capability 7.5, no RT cores and 4 GB of memory (standard NVIDIA specifications). That rules out Isaac Sim, which requires RT cores and lists 16 GB as the minimum ([Isaac Sim requirements](https://docs.isaacsim.omniverse.nvidia.com/latest/installation/requirements.html)). It sits exactly at Warp 1.18's floor of compute capability 7.5 with driver R580 or newer, and warp-lang 1.17.0 is the fallback for older drivers ([Newton GitHub](https://github.com/newton-physics/newton)). The laptop's role is CPU MuJoCo with the Apache-2.0 Menagerie FR3 model ([Menagerie](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco_menagerie)), data processing and small RL runs. The FR3 scraping and deboning policies were small enough for CPU or a single GPU.

Do all policy training on the L4. RDP fits but is tight: more than 20 GB reported for the latent diffusion stage. So do FACTR-, ACP- and HIL-SERL-sized models, which were trained on a single RTX 4090 ([FACTR arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.17432v2)), though expect longer runs on the lower-power L4. pi0 LoRA is borderline at more than 22.5 GB ([openpi](https://github.com/Physical-Intelligence/openpi)). OpenVLA-OFT's 25.6 GB training floor does not fit ([OpenVLA-OFT](https://openvla-oft.github.io/)), and neither do Dream-Tac or FAWAM. The L4 can also run batched MuJoCo Warp for the residual corrector. Isaac Lab on the L4 is plausible, since the card has RT cores and 24 GB, but the Isaac Sim requirements page does not list it.

The unresolved risk is running the policy next to the robot. RDP's latent diffusion stage takes 100 ms per inference on an RTX 4090 ([RDP arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02881v3)), and nobody has tested it on a 1650. A cloud L4 adds network latency to a contact loop, which is this report's inference rather than a measured number. Because the slow policy only needs a new chunk every 0.5 to 1 s, a slower local GPU might still keep up while the fast force decoder runs in under a millisecond. Benchmark this in week one, and ask the lab for a workstation GPU if the 1650 falls short.

## A 26-week plan that guarantees a real-robot result

The plan runs two tracks so that the simulation research can fail without sinking the thesis. The real-robot track produces an RDP-on-FR3 baseline by week 13, and that baseline is publishable on its own, because no peer-reviewed paper reports a learned force-aware Franka cucumber peeler. The simulation track adds the novel sim-to-real comparison.

| Weeks | Real-robot track | Simulation track | Gate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-3 | Lab safety; check the FR3 system version against libfranka 0.19.0; set up the real-time PC and CRISP; set collision thresholds; order the F/T sensor (about 3 weeks lead time) | Port the impedance law into a MuJoCo FR3 scene; build the RDP environment on the L4 and train on the released data | Robot holds pose under CRISP; RDP trains on the L4; inference speed known on local hardware |
| 4-6 | Adapter and fixture built; D405s mounted; load identification; scripted PCA-stroke baseline; calibration strokes with force logs and peel weights | Skin-grid peel model v0 with coverage and gouge tracking | A scripted stroke peels a real cucumber |
| 7-9 | 60 to 100 SpaceMouse demos with a force display | Fit removal thresholds to real force traces; set randomization ranges | Dataset frozen; simulated and real force traces agree on the calibration strokes |
| 10-13 | Train RDP (force) on the L4; deploy; pilot evaluation | Train the residual PPO corrector | Real baseline numbers exist |
| 14-18 | Zero-shot test of the sim corrector; optional stiffness head; F/T against FCI-estimate comparison | Randomization iterations; co-training | Sim against real comparison complete |
| 19-22 | Final evaluation: 20 trials per condition, ablations | Freeze | All tables filled |
| 23-26 | Writing, video, buffer | | Submission |

The notes point to the same time sinks every time: the real-time kernel against CUDA split, version conflicts between Polymetis, Deoxys and franka_ros, wrench frames and Jacobian conversions (RDP's own bug), interpolator rate, and calibration. These belong in weeks 1 to 6, which is why no learning happens before week 7. If the force sensor arrives late, phase 1 on the FCI estimate keeps the schedule. If the simulation track stalls, the fallback is to use simulation for controller and safety development only. That still produces a defensible thesis, because the real-robot result alone is new.

## Conclusion

This literature review changes what the project is. Asking "can a Franka peel a cucumber" is not a research question anymore: MORPHeus did it with a Panda and impedance control, and FAWAM did it with learning on an FR3. What is missing is a reproducible, open, force-aware Franka peeler, and any evidence at all on whether simulation helps peeling. RDP gives the first piece almost off the shelf. The second piece is open because the field went straight to real-world data: every peeling result to date was trained on the real robot. The sim-to-real question is therefore best framed around a narrow, vision-free residual corrector for the normal axis, where the FR3 scraping and deboning precedents say transfer works. It is not a matter of simulating the whole task.

The second insight concerns credibility more than capability. With 20 trials, the published force-aware methods are statistically indistinguishable from each other. Their metrics cannot be compared, and an outside lab's rerun of RDP scored 0.740 against the 0.95 reported in the paper. A thesis that reports every metric, adds flesh waste and coverage, compares the built-in wrench estimate with a wrist sensor on the same strokes, and puts confidence intervals on everything will be more useful to the field than a higher headline success rate. That is the kind of contribution a master's student can deliver within six months.
